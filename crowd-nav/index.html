How does a crowd flow around a robot pushing upstream through it?

A 50 m × 6 m corridor is kept full from end to end: everyone walks one way at about 1.0 m/s, re-entering at the back when they leave, so the passage stays crowded the whole clip. Driving the opposite way, against the flow, is a robot — not a controller, just an open-loop zigzag that bounces wall-to-wall at about 0.75 m/s. What varies between cards is the pedestrian model: the rule each person uses to avoid the oncoming robot and each other.

The stage is a 50 m-long, 6 m-wide walled corridor. A robot — the real MuJoCo Go2 — drives against the crowd on an open-loop zigzag that bounces wall-to-wall: it sweeps to one wall, flips direction, heads for the other, and so on, always advancing at about 0.75 m/s. The camera is fixed: centered on the corridor, it sits behind the robot and only dollies along the corridor axis to follow it — it never pans left or right, so the robot's zigzag plays out inside a steady frame while the crowd streams toward it. All four models are reactive force models, simulated live in MuJoCo: each person steers with ORCA against the other people and reacts to the robot through a named repulsive force — from no robot-force at all (ORCA — it just keeps a clearance buffer), to a Helbing pedestrian-force (Social-Force), to the SOTA Social-Robot-Force-Model (SRFM), to SRFM plus our motion-dependent gain. Collisions and min-separation in the HUD are therefore physically real.

Collisions are counted, from real geometry

A robot-pedestrian contact turns the struck person and the robot bright red and increments the HUD counter, computed from real MuJoCo contact geometry. The robot is a moving obstacle: a contact does not end the run, so you watch the whole upstream traverse.

The four pedestrian models

From pure geometry to a motion-dependent robot force — each is a different theory of how a person reacts, with its own tunable parameters.

Each model exposes a handful of knobs you can change to alter the crowd's behaviour. Two are shared by every model (the geometry of a person): ped_radius = 0.3 m (each person's disc / personal bubble) and the walking speed v_pref = 1.0 m/s. Every crowd also shares a realistic anisotropic gait — forward ≤ 1.0, sidestep and backward ≤ 0.333 m/s, with at most 0.5 m of sideways and 0.5 m of backward travel — so no model can dodge the robot by sprinting sideways or backwards. The model-specific parameters — with the exact values used in these clips — are below.

ORCA (tuned)math

Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance: each agent picks the closest velocity to its preferred one that is guaranteed collision-free for a time horizon. No special robot force. On this page ORCA uses a clearance margin so the crowd gives the non-reciprocating robot a natural berth (see Tuning ORCA); with standard params it collides far more.

\[ \mathbf{v}^{\text{new}}_A \;=\; \underset{\mathbf{v}\,\in\,ORCA^{\tau}_A}{\arg\min}\;\bigl\lVert \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\text{pref}}_A \bigr\rVert \qquad ORCA^{\tau}_A=\bigcap_{B\neq A} ORCA^{\tau}_{A\mid B} \]
Each agent A picks the velocity closest to its preferred one v_pref that still lies in ORCAτ — the intersection of the collision-free velocity half-planes, one per neighbour B, that keep it clear for the next τ seconds. No robot term: the robot is just another neighbour. Solved as a small linear program each step.
ped_radius0.3 meach person's disc radius — the clearance ORCA keeps between people (and to the robot)
v_pref1.0 m/spreferred walking speed each person steers back toward
radius_margin0.4 mthe lever: extra clearance added to the planning disc only — drops contacts 26.2→17.8 (standard: 0)
neighbor_dist4.0 mhow far out ORCA looks for others to avoid (standard value)
max_neighbors30most agents considered for a safe velocity (standard 10 — raised for dense flow)
time_horizon4.0 splan-ahead window (standard ~3; barely affects robot contacts — ORCA expects reciprocity)
Social-Forcemath

Helbing's social-force: people feel an exponential repulsion from each other and from the robot, which here is treated as just another pedestrian.

\[ m\,\dot{\mathbf{v}}_i \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{v}^{\text{pref}}_i-\mathbf{v}_i}{\tau} \;+\; \sum_{j\neq i} A\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{r-d_{ij}}{B}\Bigr)\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ij} \]
Person i accelerates back toward its preferred velocity (relaxation time τ) plus an exponential repulsion from every neighbour j (and from the robot, treated as a neighbour). A = strength, B = range/decay length, r = summed radii, dij = centre gap, n̂ij points from j to i. The repulsion grows as people get closer.
A_p2.0person↔person repulsion strength (higher = people push apart harder)
B_p0.3 mperson↔person repulsion range / decay length (higher = felt from farther)

SRFMSOTA

Social-Robot-Force-Model: the same exponential form but with a dedicated, stronger robot force (learned from the JRDB dataset), gated by whether the person can see the robot and how legible its motion is.

\[ \mathbf{f}_r \;=\; \underbrace{A_r\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d-\mathrm{dist}}{B_r}\Bigr)}_{\text{exponential robot repulsion}}\;\hat{\mathbf{n}}\;\cdot\;L \qquad A_r=7.93,\;\; B_r=0.99\ \text{m} \]
Same exponential form as social-force, but a dedicated, stronger robot term: A_r = robot-force strength (≈4× a person), B_r = its range, d = summed radii, dist = robot↔person distance, n̂ points robot→person. The whole force is gated by legibility L ∈ [0,1] — how intent-revealing the robot's motion looks.

B_r0.99 mrobot-force range / decay length
see_radius4.0 mdistance within which a person notices the robot and reacts
see_fov_deg200°field of view for noticing the robot (outside it, no reaction)
legibility L0–1scales the yield by how committed/intent-revealing the robot's motion is
Where does A_r = 7.93 come from? (it's a curve-fit, not a neural net)

A_r and B_r are just two numbers calibrated by nonlinear least-squares regression on real pedestrian trajectories in the JRDB dataset (Agrawal et al., 2024). The recipe:

① Take the two-parameter robot force above, f = A_r·exp((d−dist)/B_r)·n̂, with A_r, B_r unknown. ② For candidate values, roll the model forward and predict where each person should move as the robot passes. ③ Compare to where people actually moved in JRDB and add up the squared error. ④ Search for the (A_r, B_r) that minimise that total error — the best fit lands at A_r = 7.93, B_r = 0.99 m.

So 7.93 is the robot-repulsion strength that best reproduces how humans were observed to give way — classic parameter estimation (the same family of math as fitting a line to data), not gradient-descent network training. Our model keeps this fitted force and only multiplies it by a motion-dependent gain.

SRFM + motion gainours

SRFM's robot force plus a motion-dependent gain — the yield scales with how the robot is moving (approach angle, turn rate, time-to-collision), not just where it is. Legibility is one of three factors.

\[ \mathbf{f}_r \;=\; \mathrm{gain}\,\cdot\,A_r\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d-\mathrm{dist}}{B_r}\Bigr)\,\hat{\mathbf{n}} \qquad \mathrm{gain}=\underbrace{L_{\text{motion}}}_{\text{legibility}}\cdot\,\underbrace{A_{\text{angle}}}_{\text{approach}}\cdot\,\underbrace{U_{\text{ttc}}}_{\text{urgency}} \]
Exactly SRFM's fitted force, scaled by a motion-dependent gain built from three bounded factors — legibility L_motion, approach-angle anisotropy A_angle, and time-to-collision urgency U_ttc. Setting gain → L (i.e. λ=1, κ=0, ω=0) recovers SRFM exactly. Full factor definitions in How it works.

What the numbers say

Head-on, over 50 metres with a realistic, tightly-constrained crowd gait, only one model breaks from the pack. Our SRFM + motion gain is best on both counts — 9.4 contacts per crossing and the widest mean berth (0.43 m) — roughly halving every position-only model. The other three cluster together and high: Social-Force 17.4, tuned ORCA 17.8, SRFM 18.2. When people can only sidestep 0.5 m and never sprint aside, a fixed position-based robot force can't get them clear of a non-yielding robot in time; only the motion-dependent gain — which makes people yield early, from the robot's approach — keeps contacts down. ORCA's standard params are worst (26.2); under this gait a clearance margin only partly helps (26.2→17.8) — see Tuning ORCA below. (ORCA here is the tuned config; the other three use standard parameters.)

Why the contact counts are higher than a free-dodging crowd

Every pedestrian here moves like a real person: forward up to 1.0 m/s, but only sidesteps or shuffles backward at 0.333 m/s — and no more than 0.5 m sideways or 0.5 m behind the furthest-forward point they reached. This anisotropic gait is applied identically to all four models, so the comparison is fair — but it removes the unrealistic escape hatch of sprinting sideways or backward, which is why every model's contact count is higher (and more honest) than it would be with a free-moving crowd.

How to read these clips — and what's honest about them

Everything is a simulated human model

All four are reactive force models — ORCA for human↔human avoidance plus a named robot force — physically simulated in MuJoCo, so the contacts and min-separation are real geometry. But the pedestrians are still models, not people: the rankings are statements about these human-response models, not a claim about real crowds.

What to look for

Across a row: standard ORCA grazes the oncoming robot repeatedly (with a clearance margin it keeps a natural berth); plain Social-Force and position-only SRFM open a bubble but can't hold the constrained crowd off the robot over 50 m; our SRFM + motion gain parts the crowd early, stays selective where the position-only SRFM force degrades, and ends with the fewest contacts and the most room.

⚙

Tuning ORCA — a clearance margin helps, but can't fully fix it

ORCA was worst (26.2 contacts/crossing). Why, and how much can parameters help when the crowd can't freely step aside?

The diagnosis. ORCA assumes reciprocity — each pair of agents splits the avoidance 50/50. But the robot is a non-reciprocating open-loop zigzag, so the pedestrians only do half the avoiding and expect the robot to do the rest; it never does. A parameter sweep confirms this: planning further ahead (time_horizon) or looking wider (neighbor_dist, max_neighbors) barely helps — they assume a cooperative partner. The one lever that works is a clearance margin: the pedestrians treat the robot as a disc that needs a little more room (realistic for an unfamiliar agent that doesn't give way), so they stop cutting it so close.

How much does it help? The margin roughly halves standard ORCA's contacts (26.2 → 17.8) by stopping it cutting corners — but under the tight gait it can't open much of a berth: the tuned crowd's mean clearance is just 0.21 m, barely above standard ORCA's 0.20 m, because the people physically can't step aside. So the margin brings ORCA into the pack with Social-Force (17.4) and SRFM (18.2) — but it can't reach our motion-gain's 9.4. The reciprocity gap bites hardest exactly when people can't get out of the way, and no clearance buffer closes it:

The honest reading

The margin is a legitimate, standard ORCA safety buffer, and it does real work — it cuts standard ORCA's contacts by more than half. But it is not a smarter avoidance rule: it's “keep a bit more room and don't cut corners.” Under a realistically constrained crowd that can't step aside, that's not enough — tuned ORCA lands right in the pack with the other fixed-force models (17–18 contacts) and well short of the motion-dependent gain (9.4). So ORCA's poor score is partly a parameter artifact, but the non-reciprocity it assumes is a real limitation that a clearance buffer alone can't fix.

How “SRFM + motion gain” works

The gain is not a force — it is a multiplier that makes SRFM's existing push selective: strong exactly when the robot is bearing down on you, weak otherwise.

1 · the base SRFM force

A pedestrian feels an exponential repulsion away from the robot, scaled by a single legibility scalar L (“is the robot moving fast and straight toward its goal?”):

\[ \mathbf{f} = A_r\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d-\mathrm{dist}}{B_r}\Bigr)\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,, \qquad \text{push on the person} = L\cdot\mathbf{f} \] \[ L = \mathrm{clip}\!\Bigl(\cos\angle(\mathbf{v},\text{goal})\cdot\tfrac{\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert}{v_{\text{pref}}},\;0,\;1\Bigr) \]
A_r = 7.93, B_r = 0.99 m; n̂ points robot→person. L is SRFM's single legibility scalar — high when the robot moves fast and straight toward its goal.

That scalar L is SRFM's only handle on the robot's motion. It is blind to three things a real person actually reads: the angle the robot approaches from, its turn rate, and how soon it would hit them.

2 · the gain replaces that scalar with a product of three factors

\[ \text{push on the person} = \mathrm{gain}\cdot\mathbf{f}\,, \qquad \mathrm{gain} = \mathrm{clip}(L_{\text{motion}},0,1)\cdot A_{\text{angle}}\cdot U_{\text{ttc}} \]

L_motion — legibility  range 0 → 1

\[ L_{\text{motion}} = \cos\angle(\mathbf{v},\text{goal})\cdot\tfrac{\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert}{v_{\text{pref}}}\cdot\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{\lvert\omega\rvert}{\omega_0}\Bigr) \]
ω₀ = 1.0 rad/s — the turn-rate scale.

Committed, goal-directed motion → high; slow, sideways, or fast-spinning (illegible) → low. This is the old legibility plus the new turn-rate penalty exp(−|ω|/ω₀).

A_angle — anisotropy  range 0.4 → 1

\[ A_{\text{angle}} = \lambda + (1-\lambda)\cdot\tfrac{1+\cos\varphi}{2} \]
λ = 0.4; φ = robot heading vs the robot→person direction.

Robot driving straight at you (φ≈0) → 1; you're directly behind it (φ≈180°) → λ = 0.4. So a person in the robot's path is pushed ~2.5× harder than one it is already moving away from — it does not shove people it isn't approaching.

U_ttc — urgency  range 1 → 2

\[ U_{\text{ttc}} = 1 + \kappa\cdot\mathrm{clip}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\text{ttc}_0}{\text{ttc}}-1,\;0,\;u_{\max}\Bigr) \]
κ = 0.5, ttc₀ = 2 s, u_max = 2.

ttc = time-to-collision from the relative velocity. Far-future or diverging → 1 (no change); an imminent collision (ttc < 2 s) ramps the yield up to 2×.

Legibility is only one of the three factors — which is why the accurate name is “motion-dependent gain”, not “legibility gain”.

It contains SRFM as a special case

Set λ=1 (anisotropy off), κ=0 (urgency off) and ω=0 and the gain collapses exactly to SRFM's legibility scalar L (locked to 1e-12 in a unit test). So this isn't a different force — it's SRFM with a smarter scaling that only switches on extra, physically-grounded behaviour.

3 · worked contrast — same force, different gain

Two people 2 m from the robot, identical SRFM f:

Selective push

Person A — in the robot's path, robot driving straight and fast at them, collision in ~1 s: L_motion≈1, A_angle≈1, U_ttc≈2 → gain ≈ 2 (strong yield, early). Person B — off to the side and slightly behind, no collision course: A_angle≈0.5, U_ttc=1, L_motion≈0.7 → gain ≈ 0.35 (barely nudged). Plain SRFM pushes both ≈ the same — it only sees the 2 m distance. The gain spends the robot's influence on the person who needs to move and leaves the bystander alone.

Why that enhances SRFM

Because the push lands strongest on the close, head-on, imminent encounters — and stays gentle elsewhere — the robot earns a clean give-way exactly when it needs one, without disturbing the rest of the crowd. Over 50 m of repeated head-on meetings that selectivity compounds: SRFM + motion gain holds at ≈9.4 contacts per crossing where position-only SRFM degrades to ≈18.2 (47 vs 91 over the five scenarios). Same force budget, concentrated where it matters.

Honest caveat

The gain is a correct inductive bias — it costs nothing (recovers SRFM when its factors are off) and wins where the human response genuinely depends on robot motion (structured synthetic data, and this long-corridor contact metric). But on real robot-in-crowd data (THOR-MAGNI, SACSoN, JRDB) it statistically ties SRFM: today's research robots drive too cautiously to elicit a strong motion-dependent yield, so the signal it models — though real — is currently unrewarded on real data.
